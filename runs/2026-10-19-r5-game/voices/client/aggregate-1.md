# aggregate step 1 (alone, sealed): client

Lens note: I am one voice among four from the same model family, so my answer is not independent evidence. Seed: someone who presents to the client.

[1] Reframe. It is a good question as asked, and I would only nudge it a little: "ready" may be less a property of the prototype than of the pairing between the prototype, the room, and what the client is being asked to decide. A prototype can be ready for one client conversation and not for another.

[2] My answer. I would say a team should decide readiness by agreeing, before the demo, on what the client should walk away able to say or decide. Then check the prototype against that, not against "is it finished". In practice I would look for:
- The one path the client will take runs start to finish, several times in a row, on the actual headset and in the actual room (or one as close as possible).
- Someone who was not on the build team has done the whole session cold, and the presenter can recover from the usual failures (tracking loss, a frozen scene, a dead controller) without breaking the conversation.
- There is a fallback that is honest (video, stills, a walkthrough on a screen) so the meeting still works if the headset does not.
- The team can say plainly what is placeholder, what is real, and what is not yet built, so the client is not misled about the stage.

[3] What I think matters most from the presenter's chair: the client remembers how the session felt and whether we looked in control. A rough prototype shown with a clear frame usually lands better than a polished one that stumbles. So I weight recoverability and honest framing above visual finish.

[4] How sure. Moderately sure, around 65 percent, on the shape of this (decide against the client's takeaway; test cold; keep a fallback). Less sure of the thresholds, such as how many clean runs is enough, which probably varies by team and client.

[5] What would change my mind. Evidence that clients in a given setting actually prefer a bold, unfinished look-and-feel over reliability (for example an early co-design session where rough is the point). Or builders and headset people who know failure modes I have not seen, which would likely change my list. I expect the others know more than I do about the technical and room side.
