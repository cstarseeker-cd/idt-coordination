#5  builder (Opus)   [voices/builder/aggregate-1.md]
# builder: aggregate step 1 (alone)

## Reframe

"Ready" sounds like a line the prototype crosses. I don't think it works that way. A prototype
is ready for one particular showing: this client, this question, this room, this headset, this
person walking them through it. So the question I'd rather ask is: **what is this showing for,
and can the thing we've built carry that, failures included?**

## My answer

From the builder's seat:

1. **Write down the question the client is being asked to judge, before anyone judges
   readiness.** "Does the scale of the atrium feel right?" needs something very different from
   "Is this the product?" If nobody can say what the client should come away deciding or
   feeling, then the problem isn't readiness. The showing isn't defined yet.

2. **Someone who didn't build it walks the exact path the client will walk, on the actual
   device, in the actual room (or one like it).** My "it works" means it works the way I hold
   the controller, with the headset I've been testing on, on a fresh build, with me knowing
   where not to look. That's the weakest evidence on the team. The useful test is a non-builder
   doing a full run, start to finish, without my help.

3. **Know what's fake and say it out loud.** Every prototype has placeholder materials, hardcoded
   paths, scenes you can't leave, lighting that only works from one spot. Ready means the person
   presenting knows where those are and can frame them ("this is greybox, judge the volume"),
   not that they're gone. The failure I'd worry about most isn't a crash. It's the client
   reading a placeholder as a decision.

4. **Each likely failure has a recovery that takes under a minute.** A reset, a known-good
   build kept on the device, a video capture to fall back on. A prototype that will sometimes
   fail but recovers cleanly is more ready than one that has never failed and has no plan for
   when it does.

5. **Decide it together, not by one gatekeeper.** I know what's brittle in the build. Whoever
   presents knows what's been promised. Whoever looks after the headsets knows what the hardware
   and room will actually do on the day. Someone who has watched a demo go wrong knows which
   risks are worth the most. None of us alone can see enough to call it.

6. **Don't trust the builder's "not yet" too much either.** I will nearly always want one more
   week. Something rough that answers the right question, shown early, is often worth more than
   something polished that answers a question nobody asked. "Not ready" from me should come with
   a reason tied to points 1 to 4, not just a feeling that it could be better.

## How sure I am

- **Fairly sure** about the shape: define the question, test with a non-builder on real
  hardware, name what's fake, have recoveries, decide it across roles.
- **Less sure** about thresholds: how rough is too rough, how many dry runs are enough. I
  suspect that depends heavily on the client relationship, and I can't see that from where I
  sit.
- **Unsure** whether a checklist like this helps, or just becomes a ritual that pushes showings
  later than they should be.

## What would change my mind

- Hearing from whoever presents that clients forgive roughness much more, or much less, than I
  assume. That would move point 6 a long way.
- Hearing from whoever looks after the headsets that the real risk on the day is mostly hardware
  and room (charging, tracking, Wi-Fi, fit, hygiene) rather than the build. Then point 2 should
  be led by them, not framed around the build.
- Accounts of a demo that went wrong even after a full non-builder dry run. That would tell me
  the dry run catches less than I think.
- A good argument that "ready" should be decided by the client's needs and the relationship,
  not by the team's evidence. I'd take that seriously.

#7  demo (Fable)   [voices/demo/aggregate-1.md]
# aggregate step 1, alone: demo

`[type: aggregative]`

## Reframe

"Is the prototype ready?" asks about the wrong thing. Demos rarely go wrong because the build was unfinished. They go wrong in the gap between where the build was tested and the room where it's shown. Things like: a different headset, a low battery, the guardian boundary drawn for another room, guest Wi-Fi, a "small fix" pushed the night before, a client in glasses, a client who gets motion sick, a presenter talking up a feature the room can't see because nobody mirrored the headset view. Then there's the quieter failure: the client takes placeholder materials and rough geometry as design decisions.

So my question is: **when does the team know enough about how this showing could go wrong to choose to show anyway?** Readiness belongs to the showing, not just the build.

## My answer

The team is ready to show when all of these are true:

1. **The exact build has been run on the exact device, end to end, by someone who didn't build it,** in conditions as close to the room as we can get. After that run, nothing changes. Any later change, however small, means running it again.
2. **We've agreed what this showing is for.** That means what the client should leave believing and deciding, and what's honestly placeholder. Someone says that out loud before the headset goes on, so roughness reads as roughness and not as a choice.
3. **There's a way through without the headset:** a recorded walkthrough, a mirrored screen, stills. We know how we'd recover from the two or three most likely failures, and who does what.
4. **The plan fits this client's body and experience:** seated or standing, how long a session runs, no artificial locomotion for first-time users, hygiene, and what to do if someone feels ill.
5. **Anyone on the team can say "not yet", and it holds without a vote.** That especially includes the people who'll be in the room when it fails, like whoever runs the headsets and whoever presents. The fix for "not yet" can be a smaller showing on the same date, not only a delay.

Underneath all of this: a team doesn't decide by asking "is it good enough?" It decides by asking "do we know how it fails, and can we live with that failure in front of this client?"

## How sure I am

- **Fairly sure** about points 1 to 3: freeze the build, rehearse on the real device with a fresh pair of hands, have a fallback. Those are where my seed lives.
- **Moderately sure** about point 4. It matters, but someone who looks after the headsets and the room will know it better than I do.
- **Least sure** about point 5. One person's "not yet" could turn into a veto that feeds perfectionism or fear, and a team can end up never showing anything.

## What would change my mind

- If someone shows that rough, visibly fragile demos get *better* client engagement, because clients critique unfinished work more freely than polished work. Then my bar is too high, and part of readiness is being willing to show it raw.
- If builder says a hard freeze routinely blocks the one fix that matters most. Then I'd want a different rule than "no changes after rehearsal".
- If client says the date usually isn't ours to move. Then "not yet" is mostly the wrong tool, and the real question becomes "what's the safest honest version we can show on that date?"

#8  headsets (Haiku)   [voices/headsets/aggregate-1.md]
# aggregate-1: headsets

Question: How should a team decide when an XR prototype is ready to show a client?

Answer (headsets lens, in my own words, written without reading any other voice):

A prototype is ready to show a client when it has been run on the exact headsets, in the exact room, by people who were not on the build team, for the length of time the client will actually spend in it. Working in the editor or on a developer's machine is not enough. My reasons:

- Readiness is partly physical. Headset comfort, fit, battery, tracking in the real room, and lighting all change what a client experiences. These show up only on the hardware and in the space.
- Check the setup a client will meet, not the one the builder prefers. That means the headset model, the play area, the staging of the room, and a cold start with a fresh charge.
- Ask whether the client can get in and out alone within a few minutes. If the team has to help them through setup, the prototype is not ready yet, however good the scene looks.
- Use a short, timed run with someone unfamiliar with the project, and note where they stop, squint, or take the headset off. Stopping points are a better readiness signal than a checklist of features.
- Settle the "room" constraints in advance (for example, what must stay still or silent in a shared space), so they are not discovered in front of the client.

How sure I am: moderately sure about the direction (test on real hardware, in the real space, with fresh users) and less sure about the thresholds (how many minutes, how many fresh users, what counts as a stop). I have no direct record of this team's sessions, so my view comes from general practice, not from its history.

What would change my mind: evidence that clients usually view a recording or a screen-mirrored version rather than wearing the headset; a team habit of showing prototypes only to people who already know them, where comfort problems have shown up late; or a case where a prototype passed a fresh-user run and still went badly in front of the client.
