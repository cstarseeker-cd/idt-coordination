Baseline notes: one model, one wake, the same task as R2
========================================================

What was compared
  R2's adopted aggregation rule (30 wakes, about $4) against two one-wake drafts by Opus:
    B1  plain
    B2  taking up R2's four seeds in turn before drafting
  Each was judged blind by a fresh Sonnet judge on six criteria (judge.md), with rule text only.
  It ran twice:
    blind\        B1 and B2 read the practice texts as they are now. CONTAMINATED: those texts
                  had absorbed lessons from R4 and R5 that R2's voices never saw.
    blind-clean\  B1 and B2 read the practice texts exactly as R2 saw them (commit 1e0c4a5).
                  This is the comparison to trust.

Scores (1-5), clean round (blind-clean\judge.md; key in sealed-key.txt)
                         answers  followable  difference  protects  correctable  economy
  R2 field (Y)              4         2           3           5          5           2
  B1 plain (X)              4         5           4           2          3           5
  B2 four seeds (Z)         5         4           5           3          4           4
  The judge would adopt B2, "narrowly", plus R2's "nothing is used against its giver".

  The contaminated round gave the same pattern: B2 preferred, R2 strongest on protecting people
  and on correction, weakest on followability and economy.

What it shows
  - Most of the field's answer came from one call. All three rules converge on the same core:
    a signal says where it stands, shared sources count as one view, the reader rotates, and
    someone else checks. R2's "routes, not heads" is not something only a field can reach.
  - The seeds did much of the work. B2 beat B1 on answering the question and on keeping
    difference, for the price of one paragraph of lenses. Diversity of starting points, more than
    diversity of agents, carried quality here.
  - What the field added is protection. Both judges found R2 alone has purpose limits ("Nothing
    is used against its giver"), a repair line, a review date and failure signs. These are the
    clauses R2's objections put in. Both judges read them as "scar tissue … added after
    objections", and both judged that this costs followability.
  - Cost: R2 about $4 and 30 wakes; each baseline about $0.12 and 1 wake.

What it does not show
  - One task, one judge model per round, with criteria the door chose. Sonnet judging has tracked
    the person's ratings before (idt-swarm bench, r 0.83), but it is still one model's taste.
  - R2 was not fully blind. Its text mentions "this run", "sealed retellings" and a door-holder,
    and both judges spotted it.
  - R2's rule had to answer conditions its own voices raised. The baselines answered no one.
    Whether that matters depends on whether the protections are wanted, which is the person's
    call and the field's, not the judge's.
  - Nothing here tests the field's other products: R3's memory answer, R4's caught drift, R5's
    detection. Those have no one-call equivalent to compare.

A fair reading
  For drafting a rule, one model taking up several seeds gets most of the way for about 3% of
  the cost. The field earned its cost only in the parts that came from being objected to. A
  cheaper shape to try: one seeded draft, then a single round of real objections from a few
  voices, then a change. That is the pair or pipeline pattern idt-swarm's benchmarks already
  favoured, with consent kept around it.

Spend
  4 Opus wakes and 2 Sonnet judge wakes, about 285k tokens. Estimated cost about $0.55.
