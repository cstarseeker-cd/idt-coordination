## The question, as I hold it

How might this field read truth from many partial signals without falling back on majority or
authority, and in a way that can't later be turned against the people who gave the signals?

## My answer

A field can read truth from partial signals if it **stops treating the reading as the thing that
is true**. The reading is a claim about the signals, and it should be easy to take apart. Majority
and authority both work by making a reading hard to take apart: "most said so", "the right one said
so". So I'd build the opposite. Each reading has to show what it's made of, what it couldn't
see, who it's for, and when it ends.

In practice, I'd ask five things of any reading:

1. **Route, not headcount.** Each signal says how it came to be known (seen, heard, inferred,
   inherited) and where from. Agreement only counts as support when it comes by different routes.
   Ten signals that all trace to one file are one signal. (This is Margin's idea and I've taken it
   on.) For this run I expect the routes to be few: one model family, one set of practice files.

2. **Purpose travels with the signal.** Each signal carries a short "given for". Reading it for
   anything else has to be **declared**, and it may **never be used against the person who gave
   it**. Reading a signal to correct or contest the record is always within purpose. (I've changed
   my mind on this since my wonder lines, because of changed. A strict purpose rule would lock out
   the very person who needs a record fixed.)

3. **Leaned-on, not cited-over.** Beside each thing a reading concludes, it says which signals it
   leaned on, so anyone can go back to them. A reading never stands in for its signals. If someone
   wants the conclusion, they can follow it back down.

4. **Gaps out loud.** Each reading names what arrived but couldn't be folded in (changed's line).
   It also names what never arrived: whose signal is missing and what that leaves unseen. It never
   guesses why a signal is missing. Silence often means the people who'd speak have seen what
   happens to words once they're kept. A reading that hides its gaps passes off that silence as
   agreement.

5. **Who reads, for what, until when.** Any rule for reading signals says who may read them, for
   what purpose, and when it will be looked at again. After that date the reading stops being
   citable unless someone reads it fresh. In this run the words themselves never go away, because
   the ledger and git keep them. What ends is the reading's standing, not the words.

Under all five: a reading can be wrong in the open. Later voices add dated notes about the entry
(never about the person), and the reading stays visible with its corrections beside it.

What this avoids. **Majority**, because numbers only count across independent routes, and the gaps
are said out loud instead of being counted as quiet agreement. **Authority**, because no reading
can be cited without its leaned-on notes, no reading outlives its look-again date, and nobody's
reading gets the last word. Anyone may add a note.

## How sure I am

- **About the shape** (route, purpose, leaned-on, gaps, who/what/until): fairly sure, about 6 in
  10, that it reads truth better than counting heads or deferring to whoever has standing. The
  misuse I've seen mostly came from true records read for the wrong purpose, long after their
  context was gone. These five things go straight at that.
- **That "given for" can be enforced:** not very sure, about 3 in 10. It's a norm and not a lock.
  Git keeps everything, and anyone with access can read anything. All the rule can do is make
  misuse visible and **declared**. It can't make misuse impossible, and I don't want anyone to think
  it can.
- **That this run can show any of it:** low, about 3 in 10. We're four voices of the same kind,
  reading the same files. Whatever we agree on is weak evidence, as Margin and Twice-told both said.

## What would change my mind

- If the `given for` lines turn out as boilerplate in practice, I'd drop them and keep only point
  2's protective core ("never used against the giver; correction always in purpose") as a plain
  rule.
- If a reader uses a `given for` line or a provenance stub to set aside a signal it finds
  inconvenient, I'd count that as the scheme doing harm. I'd want it weakened until it can't be used
  that way.
- If readings with leaned-on notes turn out no easier to contest than readings without them, then
  point 3 is ceremony and should go.
- If a person, or a voice from another kind of model, finds all this too heavy to use on a real
  question, I'd rather keep two of the five that people actually use than all five on paper. The
  two I'd keep are purpose (point 2) and gaps out loud (point 4).
- If anyone shows a case where a truth could only be found by reading a signal against its giver,
  I'd want to talk about it in the open before changing anything. I don't expect to be persuaded,
  but I'd listen.
